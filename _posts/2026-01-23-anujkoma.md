We talked about everything from daily routines to seeking new experiences, from spirituality to Darwinism, from materialistic to religious life, from lazy days to backpacking adventures. We reflected on how our circle of friends has changed over time <br>
And he encouraged me to start writing. As a skilled science communicator, he is well-known for sharing scientific ideas with the general audience. Professionally, he is now a wildlife ecology research specialist. I told him I would definitely write about this trip, but I still don’t know how or what exactly to write. <br>
<br>
It was one of the best weekend -- long drive, windy camping, but deep relaxing days. <br>
<br>
We have known each other since undergrad. I think, the last time I met him was in 2015, and now it’s 2026. 
We talk occasionally about all sorts of random things, and this trip came together just as randomly. I wanted to get out of my apartment and he wanted to go for birdwatching. We had a reason to meet and go somewhere <br>
<br>
<strong> Early start: </strong> <br>
I traveled from Fayetteville, AR to Fargo, North Dakota. I planned to start early and reach to his place in the evening. I started driving super early -- around 3 am. Yes, you got it right, I wrote 3 am. Honestly speaking, I woke up at that time and realized I had not set an alarm. And I couldn’t fall back asleep because my mind started to over-think what if I turn off my alarm in sleepy mood and wake up at the regular weekend time of ~10ish am. And anyway I did not feel sleepy. So, I decided to just get up and leave.  I made some coffee, got ready, and started driving. I did not have much to pack, so it was easy to leave quickly.
<br>

<strong> Long drive: </strong> <br>
The drive itself was long but easy and straight, drive i-49 and then i-29. I spend some time in the Kansas City, MO and then at Linkon and Omaha in NE. 
The weather acted terrible, mostly after NE. I think it was after somewhere in South Dakota, it started pouring heavily. At one point, I thought it would be nice to drive with my window slightly open. I think I was going ~75mph, with driver's side window open and I suddenly heard a strange noise. Then my window stopped working. <br>
It froze halfway. I could not open it nor close the window. The window was half open. At first, I felt fun but soon the wind and rain became too intense, and I started getting wet. I took one random exit and tried to fix it by pushing and pulling the window but it made the things more worse. The window came out of its frame. I was left holding the glass, completely out of its place, the door frame. I had no idea what to do and the rain and wind was blowing like crazy. Somehow, I managed to secure/hold it temporarily in the door frame and continued driving. Finally, I reached at his place at around 10 pm. <br>
I told him that my window is not working properly and that I was worried leaving the window open overnight because it might be unsafe, someone might do something with the car. But he assured me that it would be fine, I trusted him. Parked in the roadside, with window open. The next day, the window was still half open, and nothing had happened to the car. I felt relieved, at least for a moment. <br>
<br>
<strong> Window hour: </strong> <br>
The next day, the temporary way to fix the window failed. The motor that holded the window, that helps to open/close the window was not working, and the window would not stay in its place at all. At that point, the car essentially had no functioning window. It was wide open, all the time. <br>
I asked him if he knew some mechanic, but he did not. I went to Firestone, but they had a waiting list until the end of the following week. They told me I would need to replace two parts, but the necessary component, the window motor holder, or something similar, was not available anywhere in Fargo. I would have to go to Minnesota to fix it. <br>
I was not sure what to do. <br>
We laughed it off and decided to continue the trip anyway. <br>
It will be fun, driving at 80 mph with an open window -- I said. <br>
He replied, North Dakota is safe, no need to worry about anything being stolen from the car while we are away. <br>
<br>
We headed toward Theodore Roosevelt National Park {https://www.nps.gov/thro/index.htm}. <br>
We were driving 80mph, the window was open. We had lots of things to catch up. We felt like we were shouting at each other just to share small details. The sound of wind was feeling crazy. <br>
It was fun, in some way, at least until we stopped at Walmart. Before reaching Walmart we had stopped in the rest area along the highway. We felt okay leaving the car with its window open, but at Walmart, I did not feel safe. One of us had to stay with the car just to make sure that there were no unintentional event.
He went to buy some foods and while searching for a quick fix, I came across a video that suggested using a spatula to pull the window up, and secure it with duct tape. Luckily, my friend managed to pull the glass up from deep inside the door. We taped the window glass in its place, and after that, the drive felt much safer and silence. <br>
<br>
<strong> Outlook: </strong> <br>
Nowadays, people often name places after themselves or even build statues in their own name. In contrast, I found it especially meaningful that a national park was named to honor President Theodore Roosevelt. A bit of googling made me realize that In 1883, Roosevelt traveled to the Dakota Territory to hunt bison. As a young man from New York, his experiences in the wilderness may have played a key role in shaping his lifelong commitment to conservation. Later, as president, he established the United States Forest Service (USFS) to help protect wildlife and public lands {https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/historyculture/theodore-roosevelt-and-conservation.htm}. Years afterward, this  Theodore Roosevelt national park was named in his honor in recognition of his efforts to preserve natural resources. My first glimpse of the park was from the Painted Canyon Visitor Center, located at an i-94 rest area, and the view was absolutely stunning. <br>
<br>
<strong> Endurance:  </strong> <br>
We camped at Medora Campground (https://maps.app.goo.gl/7EEoeHbSYLztUd2Y6), and I was quite fascinated by small Medora town that we saw on the way to campground. It was lively small downtown, people were out enjoying themselves at bars and local shops. People were shouting, walking on the streets. Cowboy hats, Wild West styles and it had all the energy of a small city. We were happy to see it. We told ourself that if we get time after setting up the camp, we will be back. <br>
We set up camp around 9 p.m. and decided to head to a nearby liquor store to grab a few bottles of beer. But as soon as we reached there on that small Medora downtown area, everything was closed. Not a single person was around. It felt surreal, just moments earlier the place was vibrant, and now it was completely silent. We could have driven another half hour to reach a nearby Dickinson and find some beers, but we returned to camp empty-handed. Although we were driving and didn’t have to walk a single mile, we still hesitated to drive half an hour despite our energy.
We came back at the campsite, and we started talking like the a bedtime story where the grape becomes bitter just because he didn't get it. We started joking around and telling that beer is not good for health. Our conversation drifted from chasing fun to saving vital numbers required for the smooth running of our body. We started comparing our LDL, cholesterols and so on and realized we were both hovering just below the threshold of being a chronic disease and starting medication. We laughed at ourselves. Both of us were acknowledging the change in our strength and were silently trying to address it, but we hadn't been quite successful. <br>
We remembered about the hikes we had done together back in Nepal. During one hike, we climbed from around 1,200 meters to 4,300 meters in a single push. It was raining, and we were climbing an almost vertical mountain, with of slope ~80 degree. We started early morning and made to the top of that mountain Gosainkunda in the evening, but night was scary for me. We talked about another trek where more than 60 leeches clung to his legs within an hour. I don't remember the name of that location but I remember the bloods though his legs. Back then, we were full of energy and fearless. And now here we were, reflecting on our health, even hesitating to drive less than half an hour, across flat land. <br>
<br>
<strong> Parking:  </strong> <br>
The next day we drove though the Theodore Roosevelt National Park. Although he had been there multiple times, he was enjoying the view and was scanning for the birds. For me, the landscape was fascinating. It reminded me of the Inner Terai belt of Nepal. The landscape most likely formed by the continuous soil erosion, and it looked interesting to me. The exposed different layers/horizons of the soil/rocks left behind by the wind/water erosion added unique beauty to the landscape. <br>
It was interesting to observe the landscape but I would not go there multiple times, once is enough for me. Personally, I don't have desire or the enthusiasm to count the birds or any animals. I am happy that I visited, but if I have chances I would prefer exploring to new places than returning there again. Having said that, it was fabulous place and now I wonder would it look like when there is some snow in it. <br>
<br>
<strong> Home:  </strong> <br>
Apart from the landscape, the park was home to deer, wild horses and bison. Athough I am not sure what wild horses would look like but the one that we saw were not anything like wild to me. My friend was telling me that these horses were re-introduced in this location later. I also enjoyed watching small black tailed praire dogs. For me, they were incredibly cute and looked like a small family living together. I had seen them in documentaries, and I had imagined them to be much bigger than they actually are. As we watched, we saw some people trying to listen to their sound, as if they could understand their voice and interactions. It was disappointing, to see some people getting too close to their homes, possibly trying to touch them. Honestly, I also had desire to hold them in hand and talk to them, but I guess they are not my friends :). <br>
After few meters drive inside the national park, we saw few bison. They were only a few meters far from the roadway. Personally, I didn’t find them as large as I had expected. I wished to  feel the texture of their fur and skin but they are truly wild animals and might react aggressively if approached. I don't know but I don't wanna try. 
<br>
The bison at the State museum seemed much larger. Maybe their shape and size might have changed with time, but I didn’t try to verify that. Instead, I was more interested in learning how people in the past used bison skins to build homes (tipis) and the strategies they used to hunt them. The museum had some fascinating images and stories.
<br>
I am not really a museum person. I like art museums (or art collection centers, as I prefer to call them) to some extent, but history museums are not for me. I easily get tired of them. I feel I have a lot to learn from my own life experiences. Diving deep into someone else's past does not always appeal to me. History, especially community histry, is like water under the bridge. It is already gone, and there is no need to think much about it. Even so, I respect history and the people who worked to bring society to where it is today. My friend has a strong interest in history. He encouraged me to learn more about how local tribes shaped the culture and lifestyle of the region. I got tired after a quick walk through the museum. Still, I saw detailed information about tribal life and how central bison were to it. I also realized that North Dakota holds strong cultural value for Native communities. Visiting the museum helped me understand how their role shaped the region’s traditions and identity. <br>
<br>
<strong> Native:  </strong> <br>

Please correct me if I am wrong, I think native is preferred as it is short and simple, while indigenous is used in more broader context, and American Indian is a legal term defined in the US laws. My friend was suggesting me to consistently use Native American word. I tried to challenge his idea but I was not and still is not sure of myself, so I accepted his perspective. He explained the reasoning behind it, but I am sorry, I have already forgotten the details. There were few tribal nation in the area. I was fortunate to go to xx to see the xx.
